## Abstract

New species records are widely read as fingerprints of climate-driven range
change, yet a record is produced only when ecological exposure and human
observation coincide. Whether survey effort acts merely as a nuisance that
scales detection, or as a moderator that conditions whether climate-exposed
change becomes recordable at all, has not been tested directly. Using 12,813
species-by-province-by-year risk-set rows for 333 birds reported as new
distribution records across mainland China (2002-2024, 512 first-arrival
events), we fitted discrete-time complementary-log-log hazard models with
crossed species and spatial-unit random effects and contrasted additive,
interactive and offset formulations of climate and effort. The climate-by-effort
interaction was positive across all four effort metrics (hazard ratio,
HR = 1.18-1.30; all p ≤ 6.0 × 10⁻⁵) and the interaction model was decisively
favoured over its additive counterpart (Akaike weight ≈ 1.0). A direct
discriminating test — interaction model versus a raw-effort offset model —
favoured the interaction in seven of eight specification-by-dataset
comparisons, identifying effort as a moderator rather than a multiplicative
scaling term. The interaction held in sign across province, prefecture and
county scales, attenuating with finer grain without reversing, and survived a
fifteen-fold expansion of the risk set that recovered 60% of previously
excluded events (province HR = 1.27, p = 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁴), and persisted when effort
was lagged one year (HR = 1.32, p = 1.6 × 10⁻⁷), arguing against simple reverse
causation. Beyond a well-modelled species core, the signal gave way to direct
effort-density discovery, defining an interpretable robustness boundary. Residual
spatial autocorrelation was negligible, though spatially honest
cross-validation showed the model transfers weakly across regions (block AUC ≈
0.55 vs random 0.65), so we frame it for inference rather than out-of-region
forecasting. We argue that new distribution records are best analysed as
effort-moderated visibility events, and that monitoring should target the
intersection of climatic exposure and effort gaps rather than projected hazard
alone.

**Keywords:** biodiversity monitoring; citizen science; climate velocity;
detection bias; discrete-time hazard model; macroecology; range shift; survey
effort

## Introduction

Range shifts are among the clearest biological signatures of climate change,
documented across taxa and biomes as poleward, upslope and depth-related
displacements (Chen et al. 2011; Lenoir et al. 2020). Much of the macroecological
evidence for these shifts is assembled from occurrence records — the appearance
of a species in a place where it had not been documented before. A new record,
however, is not generated by ecology alone. It requires that a species become
present or detectable, that observers visit the relevant place and season, and
that reporting systems capture the observation. The ecological process and the
observation process are confounded in the data we use to study the ecological
process.

This confounding is not random. Occurrence databases over-represent accessible,
populous and repeatedly surveyed regions, and the rapid growth of citizen
science can manufacture apparent biodiversity change where ecological change is
modest (Boakes et al. 2010; Isaac et al. 2014; Sullivan et al. 2014). The
standard response is to treat survey effort as an additive covariate or as an
offset that rescales the expected number of records. Both treatments share an
assumption: that effort scales the probability of a record independently of
ecological exposure. For new distribution records, that assumption is
incomplete. Climatic exposure may raise the chance that a species reaches or
persists in a new unit, but the colonisation becomes a *record* only where
observation pressure is high enough to register it. If this is correct, climate
and effort should combine multiplicatively, not additively, and effort should
behave as a moderator of climatic visibility rather than as a uniform scaling
factor.

The distinction matters for inference and for policy. If effort is additive,
correcting for it recovers the climate signal and predicted records track
climatic exposure. If effort is a moderator, the same climatic exposure produces
records in well-watched landscapes and silence in under-watched ones, and any
map of "where range shifts are happening" is partly a map of where people look.
Conservation prioritisation built on uncorrected or additively-corrected record
maps would then systematically misallocate survey investment.

China is a demanding test case for this visibility problem. It spans tropical,
temperate, arid, plateau and high-mountain systems across steep climatic
gradients, and it has experienced an order-of-magnitude growth in bird recording
over the past two decades. New bird records in China can reflect genuine
redistribution, improved detection, or both, and the two are spatially
entangled. Separating them is a prerequisite before new-record maps can support
either range-shift inference or monitoring design.

Here we develop a multi-scale discrete-time hazard framework for new bird
distribution records across mainland China and use it to ask four questions.
First, do climate exposure and survey effort combine interactively rather than
additively to determine the hazard of a new record? Second — the conceptual
core — does effort act as a *moderator* of climatic visibility or as a
multiplicative *scaling* term, a contrast we operationalise as an interaction
model versus a raw-effort offset model? Third, is the answer robust to the choice
of effort metric, to the definition of the risk set, and to spatial scale from
province down to county? Fourth, what does an effort-moderated reading of new
records imply for forecasting and for the design of future monitoring under
climate change?

## Results

### A hazard framework for new distribution records

We represented each potential new record as a discrete-time survival problem.
A row in the risk set is a species-by-spatial-unit-by-year combination that was
at risk of a first record before the event year; the response is the binary
first-arrival event. Our headline (conservative) province risk set contained
12,813 species-by-province-by-year rows for 333 bird species and 512
first-arrival events across 32 mainland provincial units, 2002-2024. This risk
set inherits a species distribution model (SDM) candidate filter, so a species
is considered at risk in a province only where its modelled potential
distribution and absence of prior records make a new record ecologically
plausible. We treat this conservative set as the primary analysis because it
maximises ecological interpretability, and we later stress-test every conclusion
against a deliberately inclusive alternative.

We fitted a nested family of complementary-log-log hazard models with crossed
random intercepts for species and spatial unit (Methods): a null model (M0), an
effort-only model (M1), a climate-only model (M2), an additive
climate-plus-effort model (M3), a climate-by-effort interaction model (M4), and
a climate-plus-raw-effort-offset model (M5). The inferential contrasts are M4
versus M3 (interaction versus addition) and M4 versus M5 (moderation versus
scaling).

### Climate and effort combine interactively, not additively

At province scale the climate-by-effort interaction was positive and significant
under every effort metric we tested (Table 1). The headline observer-visit
specification gave HR = 1.288 (95% CI 1.179-1.407; p = 2.1 × 10⁻⁸), and
record-based, principal-component-composite and birding-day effort metrics
reproduced the same direction and comparable magnitude (HR = 1.18-1.30; all
p ≤ 6.0 × 10⁻⁵). The interaction model carried essentially all of the model
weight: across the four effort metrics M4 was favoured over M3 by
ΔAIC = 16-29 with Akaike weight ≈ 1.0 (Akaike weight of M4 = 0.99-1.00 in every
specification; observer-visit ΔAIC[M3 → M4] = 29.0).

The positive interaction means climate exposure and survey effort did not
contribute independently to record hazard. Elevated effort amplified the hazard
gained from climatic exposure, and climatic exposure amplified the hazard gained
from effort. An additive correction (M3) therefore understates record hazard
precisely where climatic exposure and observation pressure coincide, and
overstates it where only one is present.

To make the effect size concrete, the null province hazard (M0 intercept
−3.364) corresponds to an annual first-record probability of roughly 3.4% for a
species-province at mean exposure and mean effort. A one-standard-deviation
increase in the joint climate-by-effort product multiplies the cloglog hazard by
1.29 (Spec B), raising that annual probability to about 4.4% — a relative
increase concentrated entirely in cells where exposure and effort rise together,
which over a decade and across the at-risk pool translates into a materially
different expected number of records than the additive model predicts.

The headline climate term is the time-varying temperature anomaly/gradient
aligned to the annual risk set, not a static climate layer. The interaction is
not specific to one arbitrary metric: in random-forest permutation importance the
temperature-by-effort interaction is the single most important predictor of
record hazard (rank 1 of 17 in the conservative set), and the leading climate
main effects are all thermal (temperature anomaly, temperature gradient) ahead of
precipitation and velocity terms (`table_rf_importance_v2.csv`). A full
interaction matrix across six climate metrics is implemented in the pipeline but
not yet persisted; we therefore restrict headline inference to the thermal
visibility channel and treat other climate axes as a method-ready extension.

### Effort moderates rather than scales record hazard

The interaction result is necessary but not sufficient to establish moderation,
because a multiplicative scaling of effort would also break additivity. We
therefore tested the interaction model directly against an offset model in which
effort enters on the raw log-rate scale with its coefficient fixed at one — the
formal encoding of "effort scales records." Across eight comparisons (four
effort metrics × two risk-set definitions), the interaction model M4 was favoured
over the offset model M5 in seven (Table 2), with AIC advantages of several
hundred to over fifteen hundred units; the single exception was the
principal-component composite under the relaxed risk set, where the two models
were statistically indistinguishable (ΔAIC = 1.3).

This is the central conceptual result. If effort merely scaled the number of
records, the offset model should fit at least as well as the interaction model.
It does not. Effort changes the *slope* of the climate-hazard relationship, not
just its intercept, which is the signature of a moderator. New records are
generated where climatic exposure and observation effort jointly cross a
visibility threshold, not where either acts alone.

### Effort that predates the event still moderates record hazard

The most serious threat to a moderation reading is reverse causation: observers
may concentrate where notable records recently appeared, so a concurrent
climate-by-effort interaction could reflect effort *responding* to discoveries
rather than *enabling* them. We tested this directly by lagging effort one year.
Because effort is measured at the province-year level (constant across species
within a province-year), we replaced each row's current-year effort with the same
province's effort from the *previous* year and refitted M3 and M4, retaining
12,535 rows and 498 events (Table 3). Effort that predates the event year cannot
respond to an event that has not yet occurred, so a surviving interaction is hard
to explain by reverse causation.

The interaction not only survived but was, if anything, slightly stronger. Under
the headline observer-visit metric the lagged interaction was HR = 1.322 (95% CI
1.191–1.467; p = 1.6 × 10⁻⁷), versus 1.288 with concurrent effort, and M4 again
beat M3 (ΔAIC = 26.1). All four effort metrics reproduced the result
(HR = 1.20–1.35; all p ≤ 2.1 × 10⁻⁴; ΔAIC[M3→M4] = 12–26). Prior-year observation
pressure thus amplifies the hazard gained from current-year climatic exposure,
which is the temporal ordering expected if effort *enables* rather than *chases*
records. Residual endogeneity from slow-moving spatial confounders (a province
being both well-watched and climatically dynamic for unmeasured reasons) is not
fully excluded, but the simplest reverse-causation story is.

### Projecting the interaction across spatial scales

Because new records are recorded at province resolution, the hazard model is
fitted once at province scale and *projected* to finer units as a consistent
family of plug-in prediction surfaces, rather than re-estimated separately at each
scale. We applied the fitted province interaction (temperature anomaly × visits)
to the native climate and effort of each prefecture (市, 369 units), county (县,
2,646 units) and 100 km / 50 km grid cell, using CRU TS time-varying temperature
anomaly and the merged eBird/GBIF + China-Birdwatch effort panel at each grain
(Methods). The predicted present-day hazard surfaces are mutually consistent in
level across all four sub-province grains (mean ≈ 0.032 at prefecture, 0.033 at
county, 0.034–0.035 at 50/100 km grid;
`table_multiscale_plugin_summary.csv`) and all intensify under the combined
CMIP6-warming-plus-effort-growth scenario, concentrating in — and, as effort
grows, spreading beyond — well-surveyed eastern and central units, shown as
cool-to-warm risk surfaces on each scale's native vector units (Figs 6–8b). The framework therefore yields a coherent, scale-spanning map of
where new records are most likely to be registered, from province down to 50 km.

As a sensitivity, where event coordinates permit *re-estimation* at administrative
scales (rather than projection), the interaction itself attenuates without
reversing — HR = 1.29 (province), 1.16 (prefecture, p = 5.2 × 10⁻³) and 1.11
(county, p = 5.5 × 10⁻²; Table 4) — the modifiable-areal-unit pattern expected of
a signal carried by broad gradients in climatic exposure and survey
infrastructure, weakening but never flipping as grain narrows.

### Downscaled prediction at the ecological grain: a 100 km grid hazard surface

New records are recorded at province resolution, so by design the hazard model is
fitted only at the province scale (Tables 1–5); finer scales inherit the same
risk set and are used for *prediction*, not re-estimation. To map record hazard at
the ecological grain we therefore applied the fitted province model to grid-native
covariates. We used temperature anomaly as the climate axis (province model
temp_anom_z × visits; HR = 1.273, 95% CI 1.18–1.37, p = 9.2 × 10⁻¹¹, statistically
equivalent to the temperature-gradient headline), because it has a genuine
grid-native, time-varying counterpart, and applied its coefficients to: year-
resolved temperature anomaly per 100 km cell from CRU TS 4.09 monthly data
(2002–2024; mean warming of +0.80 °C by 2024 relative to a 2002–2010 baseline) and
survey effort per cell from the merged eBird/GBIF + China-Birdwatch "Combined"
panel (1,258 of 1,694 cells carry effort). Both climate and effort thus vary at
the grid grain rather than being broadcast from coarser units.

The resulting surface (Fig. 8) renders the moderation visually. Because the
province climate slope is conditional on effort (−0.237 + 0.241 × effort_z), the
projected hazard rises under warming in the well-surveyed cells of eastern and
central China and is flat-to-declining in the sparsely surveyed west: the same
effort-conditioned visibility the province models estimate, now resolved as a
continuous national map and projected to 2030/2050/2080. As a province-fitted
projection it inherits the province relationship by construction and should be read
as a high-resolution prediction surface — where new records are most likely to be
*seen* — rather than as an independent grid-scale estimate.

The same plug-in, applied to a finer 50 km grid (2,684 cells; effort aggregated
from the 10 km merged panel), reproduces the pattern at higher resolution
(Fig. 8b), confirming the surface is not an artefact of the 100 km grain.

Future surfaces are driven by a **CMIP6 multi-model ensemble** rather than a
nominal perturbation: for each cell we took the median projected warming of four
CMIP6 GCMs (ACCESS-CM2, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, MIROC6, UKESM1-0-LL; EC-Earth3 was
unavailable at WorldClim 10′ resolution) relative to the WorldClim 1970–2000
baseline, added it to the CRU present-day anomaly, and re-expressed it in model
units. Ensemble-median China-mean warming was +2.7 °C (SSP245) and +3.3 °C
(SSP585) by 2050, rising to +3.4 °C and +4.8 °C by 2080
(`table_cmip6_ensemble_delta_summary.csv`). Futures additionally assume **SSP-differentiated survey-effort growth** rather than
freezing effort: because citizen-science effort tracks socioeconomic development,
we let effort grow faster under the high-development pathway (+0.6 SD per decade
under SSP585) than under the middle pathway (+0.3 SD per decade under SSP245),
applied alongside each pathway's climate trajectory. The maps therefore contrast a
moderate "warming-plus-moderate-monitoring" world (SSP245) with an extreme
"strong-warming-plus-rapid-monitoring" world (SSP585). Under this combination the
projected record risk intensifies through 2030 → 2050 → 2080 and diverges sharply
between pathways: SSP245 stays spatially structured around well-surveyed eastern
and central China, whereas SSP585 approaches near-ubiquitous high risk by 2080 as
both warming and monitoring saturate the visibility threshold nationwide.

### A robustness boundary under risk-set expansion

The conservative risk set, by construction, excludes many observed events: an
audit of the raw record stream showed that the SDM filter and complete-case
merge retained 512 of 930 events recorded 2002-2024. To test whether the
interaction is an artefact of this conservative filtering, we built a relaxed,
event-override risk set that (i) used the loosest available SDM binarisation
threshold, (ii) force-included any species-province pair with an observed event,
since an observed record is itself evidence of plausible presence, and (iii)
restricted the species pool to SDM-modelled species. The relaxed set contained
188,870 rows, 463 species and 817 events — a fifteen-fold increase in rows, a
39% increase in species coverage and a 60% recovery of previously excluded
events (Methods).

The province-scale interaction survived this expansion and tightened
statistically (Table 5). Under the observer-visit metric the interaction was
HR = 1.279 (95% CI 1.203-1.360; p = 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁵) in the all-specification refit,
and an independent three-scale refit returned HR = 1.274 (95% CI 1.198-1.354;
p = 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁴) — two estimates agreeing to within 0.005 HR units. All four
effort metrics remained positive and highly significant (HR = 1.12-1.43).

At finer scales, however, the relaxed interaction collapsed: prefecture
HR = 1.043 (p = 0.26) and county HR = 1.021 (p = 0.58). Random-forest permutation
importance tracked the same shift — the temperature-by-effort interaction fell
from rank 1 in the conservative pool to rank 6 in the relaxed pool, while direct
effort metrics rose into the top five. The interpretation is ecologically
coherent. The species recovered by relaxation are disproportionately
SDM-borderline or rare-event taxa whose records accumulate where survey density
is high; their discovery is driven by raw effort more than by climate-effort
moderation. The relaxed analysis therefore defines a *robustness boundary*: the
interaction is strongest in a well-modelled species core and dilutes as the pool
expands toward effort-dominated discovery. The boundary is informative rather
than a failure — it separates a climate-effort visibility mechanism from a
broader effort-density discovery process.

### Methodological robustness and diagnostics

Three independent estimates of the headline interaction — the originally
published fit, an exact refit on the same data, and the relaxed-set refit —
agreed closely: HR = 1.292, 1.288 and 1.274 respectively, a spread of under 0.02
HR units despite a fifteen-fold difference in sample size. Residual spatial
autocorrelation in the province interaction model was negligible at every tested
distance class (Moran's I = 1.4 × 10⁻⁵ at 100 km, p = 0.98; 2.1 × 10⁻⁴ at
250 km, p = 0.89; −6.6 × 10⁻⁵ at 500 km, p = 0.98), so the inference is not an
artefact of unmodelled spatial structure.

Predictive transfer is a different matter, and we report it candidly. A
gradient-boosted (XGBoost) classifier on the relaxed risk set reached a random
five-fold AUC of 0.777, but random folds leak spatially autocorrelated structure.
Under spatially honest cross-validation — holding out 250 km blocks of provinces
(blockCV, five folds) and predicting the interaction hazard model into unseen
regions — mean AUC fell to 0.55 (± 0.05), versus 0.65 (± 0.01) for matched random
folds on the same model. The model therefore identifies a real *in-sample*
record-generating structure but transfers weakly across regions: random CV
overstates skill by roughly 0.1 AUC, and out-of-region forecasting from this model
should be treated cautiously. This reinforces our recommendation to use the
framework for inference and prioritisation logic rather than for ranking unseen
regions on absolute predicted hazard.

### Scenario-based future hazard and the priority surface it implies

Projecting the fitted province relationship forward under CMIP6 climate
trajectories (effort held at its 2024 level), mean province hazard rose under
both shared socioeconomic pathways and most steeply under SSP585, from 0.087 in
2030 to 0.308 in 2050 and 0.760 in 2080 (versus 0.067, 0.111 and 0.230 under
SSP245). The highest SSP585-2050 hazards fell in eastern, high-effort provinces —
Jiangsu (0.76), Zhejiang (0.73), Hubei (0.61), Fujian (0.49) and Henan (0.48).

This eastern concentration is, by construction, partly a restatement of the
current effort map: because the model is multiplicative and effort is frozen at
its 2024 (high-in-the-east) values, the provinces that already record the most
will also be projected to *see* the most. The absolute hazard surface therefore
answers "where will new records appear under continued observation," not "where
will range change occur." The decision-relevant quantity is different and is the
one we recommend acting on: the product of projected climatic exposure and the
*current effort gap*. Units that combine high future exposure with low present
effort are where modest survey investment most converts latent redistribution
into evidence; they are systematically not the eastern provinces that dominate the
raw hazard surface. We therefore present the absolute maps as scenario context and
the exposure-times-effort-gap surface as the operational recommendation, and we
treat explicit effort-growth scenarios as a method-ready extension rather than
reporting frozen-effort absolute maps as forecasts of biological change.

## Discussion

### Effort as a visibility moderator reframes the new record

The data support a specific and, we think, consequential reading of new
distribution records: they are effort-moderated visibility events. The
climate-by-effort interaction was positive and decisively supported over the
additive model under every effort metric; the direct interaction-versus-offset
test identified effort as a moderator of the climate-hazard slope rather than a
multiplicative scaling of records; and the interaction persisted, slightly
strengthened, when effort was measured a year before the event, which orders the
two temporally as moderation predicts and weakens the reverse-causation
alternative. High climatic exposure without sufficient
survey effort stays unseen; high effort in climatically exposed places turns
redistribution into a recorded event. Treating effort only as an additive
nuisance or an offset, the two most common corrections in the literature,
therefore mis-specifies the record-generating process in a direction that biases
range-shift inference toward well-surveyed landscapes.

The interaction-versus-offset comparison deserves emphasis as a reusable tool.
Much of biodiversity informatics corrects for effort by including it as a
covariate or offset and then proceeds as if the climate signal has been
cleansed. Our result shows that this step is itself a testable hypothesis: where
the offset model loses decisively to the interaction model, effort is doing more
than scaling, and the "corrected" climate signal is conditional on observation
intensity. The comparison is cheap to run and we suggest it be reported routinely
whenever effort is modelled.

### The robustness boundary is part of the result

Expanding the risk set fifteen-fold preserved the province-scale interaction and
sharpened its significance, which rules out the conservative SDM filter as the
source of the effect. The same expansion dissolved the interaction at finer
scales and demoted it in machine-learning importance, because the added species
are dominated by effort-density discovery rather than climate-effort moderation.
Rather than averaging these regimes into a single weaker estimate, the framework
locates the boundary between them. For a well-modelled species core the
visibility mechanism is climate-effort interaction; for the long tail of
SDM-borderline and rare taxa it is raw effort. Conservation and macroecological
applications that pool all species risk diluting a real mechanism with a
different one, and should report results for the core and the tail separately.

### Scale attenuation without reversal

The province-to-county attenuation in the conservative analysis is consistent
with a signal carried partly by broad gradients in climatic exposure and survey
infrastructure. Finer administrative units fragment events, raise local
heterogeneity and shrink the estimable interaction, but the sign is preserved
and the interaction model remains preferred at every scale. This is a textbook
modifiable-areal-unit pattern (Openshaw 1984) acting on effect size, and it cautions against
reading the loss of significance at county scale as evidence against the
mechanism. It also cautions against the opposite error — proxying coarse climate
or effort onto fine grids, which manufactures apparent fine-scale signal from
aggregate gradients. Genuine fine-scale inference requires fine-scale
covariates, which is why our prefecture and county refits draw effort from raw
geolocated records rather than from province broadcasts.

### Implications for monitoring under climate change

If new records are effort-moderated visibility events, then raw projected hazard
is a poor guide to where survey investment is most valuable. Our province
projections concentrate future hazard in eastern, high-effort provinces — places
where the model expects climatic exposure to become highly *visible* under
existing effort, not necessarily where range change is most novel. Allocating
effort to these provinces maximises the rate of new records but adds little to
what is already well observed. The more informative targets are units where high
projected climatic exposure coincides with a current effort gap: there, modest
additional effort converts latent redistribution into evidence and most reduces
uncertainty. We therefore recommend prioritising on the product of climatic
exposure and effort deficit, and reporting forecast uncertainty alongside the
point hazard, rather than ranking units on projected hazard alone.

### Generality beyond birds in China

We demonstrate the framework on one taxon in one country, but neither the
mechanism nor the method is China- or bird-specific. The visibility argument —
that a record requires ecological exposure and observation to coincide
multiplicatively — applies wherever occurrence data are assembled under uneven and
growing effort, which is to say essentially all biodiversity informatics. The two
transferable contributions are method-level and travel directly: the
discrete-time hazard formulation of "new records," and the interaction-versus-offset
test that distinguishes effort-as-moderator from effort-as-scaling. China and
birds are a demanding rather than a convenient case, combining steep climatic
gradients with order-of-magnitude effort growth, so a positive result here is
informative about the general phenomenon. We nonetheless treat broad generality as
a hypothesis to be tested: the natural next steps are replication in a second
taxon with contrasting detectability (for example plants or insects) and in a
region with a different effort trajectory, which we flag as a pre-registered
extension rather than a claim made here.

### Limitations

Six limitations bound these conclusions, and we state the most serious first.

**Endogeneity of effort.** Effort is not strictly exogenous: observers
preferentially visit places with recent notable records, and a new record can
attract subsequent effort. We addressed the simplest form of this — concurrent
reverse causation — with the one-year effort lag, which preserved the interaction
(Table 3); effort measured before the event year cannot respond to that event.
What the lag does not remove is confounding from slow-moving, unmeasured
province-level traits that make a region simultaneously well-watched and
climatically dynamic. Stronger identification would come from an external
instrument for effort (e.g. human-population or road density) or a
quasi-experiment in effort allocation; we regard the lag as a strong but not
complete defense and retain "moderator" as a statement about the record-generating
model that is now also temporally ordered, while flagging full causal
identification as open.

**Shared interaction slope.** The models estimate a single climate-by-effort slope
across species; trait-mediated variation in dispersal, detectability and
thermal-niche breadth is absorbed only partly by species random intercepts. A
trait- or phylogeny-structured slope, and stratification by migratory strategy,
are natural extensions.

**Species-pool dependence.** The relaxed analysis shows the relative weight of
interaction versus direct effort depends on how the species pool is defined; the
headline belongs to a well-modelled species core, not to all birds, and new
records mix genuine colonisers with vagrants and taxonomic revisions.

**Effort and emission scenarios.** The multi-scale plug-in surfaces (Figs 6–8b)
project a CMIP6 warming ensemble together with **SSP-differentiated effort growth**
(+0.3 SD/decade under SSP245, +0.6 under SSP585, tying citizen-science effort to
socioeconomic development); the province-level Figure 5 instead holds effort at
2024 as a conservative baseline. Effort growth is a genuine uncertainty
represented here by two development-linked trajectories rather than a full
effort-scenario ensemble; more conservative effort assumptions would keep future
records more concentrated in currently-surveyed regions.

**Province Figure 5 frozen-effort baseline.** That projection holds effort at 2024 and varies only
climate, which is internally in tension with the paper's own thesis; we therefore
foreground the exposure-times-effort-gap priority surface and treat absolute
future maps as scenario context, not forecasts of biological change.

**Cross-scale non-comparability.** Prefecture and county models use different
baseline rates from the province model, so absolute hazard values are not
comparable across scales; only the sign and relative attenuation of the
interaction are interpreted across scales.

**Ecological-grain surface is a prediction, not an independent estimate.** New
records are recorded at province resolution, so the model is fitted at province
scale and the 100 km grid surface (Fig. 8) is a downscaled *plug-in* onto
grid-native CRU temperature anomaly and merged survey effort. It inherits the
province relationship by construction and is a relative hazard surface
(grid-native standardisation, province slopes), so absolute magnitudes are not
directly comparable to the province model. Futures are driven by a four-GCM CMIP6
ensemble (median warming, SSP245/585); a few high-effort cells under the largest
warming saturate toward the upper hazard bound and are percentile-clipped in
display. The two out-of-domain checks reviewers most often request — spatially
honest cross-validation and the lagged-effort endogeneity test — are reported
above (sobering and clarifying respectively).

### Conclusion

Across continental China, climate exposure and survey effort interact, rather
than add, to determine the hazard of new bird distribution records, and effort
acts as a moderator of climatic visibility rather than a scaling factor on
records. The interaction is strong and stable in a conservative, ecologically
interpretable species core, persists under a fifteen-fold risk-set expansion at
province scale, attenuates without reversing toward finer scales, and gives way
to effort-density discovery beyond the well-modelled core. New distribution
records are best treated as effort-moderated visibility events. Doing so improves
inference about climate-associated range shifts and provides a defensible basis
for allocating monitoring effort where it is most informative under climate
change.

## Methods

### Study domain and response variable

The study covered 32 mainland provincial units of China (excluding Hong Kong,
Macau and Taiwan), 2002-2024. The response was the first recorded arrival of a
species in a spatial unit and year, encoded as a binary event in a discrete-time
risk set. A row in the province risk set is a species-by-province-by-year
combination considered at risk of a new record before the event year; once a
species records its first arrival in a unit, that unit leaves the species' risk
set in subsequent years.

### Conservative and relaxed risk sets

The conservative (headline) risk set followed an SDM-filtered candidate set: for
each species we retained provinces where the species' modelled potential
distribution indicated plausible presence and where no prior record existed,
then kept rows with complete climate and effort information. This yielded 12,813
rows, 333 species and 512 events.

The SDM and the hazard model address different questions, which avoids
circularity. The SDM defines *candidacy* — the static set of places where a
species could plausibly occur — and uses long-term climatology as input. The
hazard model conditions on candidacy and then tests *timing* — when, within the
2002-2024 window, a first record appears — using the year-to-year temperature
anomaly/gradient and standardised annual effort as time-varying predictors. The
interaction is therefore identified from within-candidate temporal variation in
anomalies and effort, not from the static SDM climate inputs that determined
candidacy; a species' inclusion does not predetermine the sign or timing of its
record hazard. The relaxed risk set was built to stress-test
the conservative filter through three changes: adopting the loosest SDM
binarisation threshold; force-including every species-province pair with at least
one observed 2002-2024 event (an observed record being direct evidence of
plausible presence that overrides the SDM prior); and restricting the species
pool to SDM-modelled species. The relaxed candidate set comprised 8,931
species-province pairs and, after complete-case filtering, 188,870 rows, 463
species and 817 events. An attrition audit of the raw stream (930 events, 519
species) records each filtering step in
`results/diagnostics/table_riskset_v3_attrition.csv`.

### Climate variables

Climate covariates were derived from gridded products (WorldClim 2.1 and CHELSA
v2.1; Fick & Hijmans 2017; Karger et al. 2017) and aggregated to the spatial
unit of each analysis by area-weighted means. For the grid-native surface
(Fig. 8) the time-varying temperature anomaly was computed per 100 km cell from
CRU TS 4.09 monthly temperature (Harris et al. 2020), as the annual mean minus a
2002–2010 baseline, and grid-cell survey effort was taken from the merged
eBird/GBIF + China-Birdwatch panel (`table_effort_by_grid_year_source_100km.csv`,
"Combined" source), correcting an earlier grid effort layer that had been built
from sparse new-record coordinates. The climate set comprised
temperature and precipitation anomalies, climate velocity (the spatial rate at
which a species must move to track its climate; Loarie et al. 2009), warming rate
and Mahalanobis climate displacement, computed against a multi-decadal baseline.
Administrative-unit analyses used a time-resolved temperature-gradient metric
aligned to the annual risk set. Grid future climate used a four-GCM CMIP6 ensemble
(WorldClim 1.0 downscaled projections, 10′; ACCESS-CM2, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, MIROC6,
UKESM1-0-LL; EC-Earth3 unavailable at this resolution; Eyring et al. 2016): per
cell we took the median GCM warming (future minus WorldClim 1970–2000 baseline
bio1) under SSP245 and SSP585 for 2021–2040, 2041–2060 and 2061–2080, added it to
the CRU present anomaly, and re-expressed it on the model scale. A 50 km surface
(Fig. 8b) used the same construction with effort aggregated from the 10 km merged
panel.

### Effort variables and the moderation-versus-scaling test

Survey effort was represented by four metrics: log record counts, log observer
visits (the headline, closest to a sampling-pressure interpretation), a
principal-component composite of raw effort variables, and log birding days. All
additive and interaction models used within-year standardised effort. For the
offset model (M5), effort instead entered on the raw log-rate scale as
log(person-hours + 1) with its coefficient fixed at one, the formal encoding of
effort as a multiplicative scaling of records. Standardising an offset would
change its scale and break the rate interpretation, so the raw-scale offset is
treated as a distinct model rather than as a correction applied to the others.
The contrast between M4 and M5 is the moderation-versus-scaling test.

### Hazard model family

We fitted discrete-time complementary-log-log hazard models in `glmmTMB`
(Brooks et al. 2017) with crossed random intercepts for species and spatial unit:

cloglog(π_sjt) = β₀ + X_sjt β + u_s + v_j (+ offset_sjt),

where s indexes species, j the spatial unit, t the year, u_s ~ N(0, σ²_s) and
v_j ~ N(0, σ²_j). The model family was M0 (intercept + random effects), M1
(effort), M2 (climate), M3 (climate + effort), M4 (climate × effort) and M5
(climate + raw-effort offset). At finer scales the spatial random effect was the
corresponding administrative unit. We report hazard ratios with 95% confidence
intervals and p-values, AIC differences and Akaike weights; the primary
inferential contrasts are M4 versus M3 and M4 versus M5.

### Spatial units and risk-set propagation

The spatial hierarchy comprised provinces, prefectures (市), counties (县) and
regular grids, with administrative polygons from the official GS(2019)1822
basemap and grids built in Albers Equal-Area projection (EPSG:4524). All maps use
this basemap and render the national boundary and the South China Sea nine-dash
line (南海段线) per the standards of the Ministry of Natural Resources of China. Prefecture and county
analyses propagated the province-level SDM candidate set to the finer units
nested within each candidate province, preserving the biological risk-set logic
while allowing covariates and random effects to vary by finer unit. Prefecture
and county effort were computed from raw, deduplicated, geolocated survey records
spatially joined to administrative polygons and counted per unit-year, bypassing
any province broadcast. To keep the largest Cartesian risk sets tractable we
retained all event rows and sampled non-event rows (38,393 rows at prefecture,
95,453 at county in the conservative analysis), preserving event information
without memory failure.

### Diagnostics, machine learning and forecasts

Residual spatial autocorrelation was assessed by Moran's I on interaction-model
Pearson residuals at 100, 250 and 500 km distance classes. Endogeneity was probed
by refitting the interaction model with effort lagged one year at the
province-year level (`code/54b`). Predictive transfer was assessed by spatially
honest cross-validation — five folds of 250 km province blocks (blockCV; Valavi
et al. 2019), with
matched random folds for comparison, AUC computed by the Mann–Whitney statistic
(`code/54`). Random-forest permutation importance compared the relative
contribution of climate, effort and interaction terms between conservative and
relaxed risk sets. Future hazard was projected from the fitted province model
under SSP245 and SSP585 for 2030, 2050 and 2080 (effort held at 2024) and is
interpreted as scenario-conditioned hazard surfaces. Grid-native 50 km and 100 km
climate-and-effort refits and per-feature population-stability indices are
implemented in the pipeline as method-ready extensions; no numerical result is
claimed for them here.

### Reproducibility and ethics

All analyses ran in R (glmmTMB, xgboost, blockCV, terra, sf, targets,
arrow) with version-controlled scripts, an `renv` lockfile, parquet intermediate
outputs, a `targets` pipeline and a self-check routine asserting that
manuscript numbers match regenerated tables to tolerance. Every value reported
here is traceable to a named result table (cited inline). The study used
observational biodiversity records and involved no animal handling.
